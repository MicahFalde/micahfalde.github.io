DECISION 01No fine-tuning.

The house voice lives in a folder of text, fetched at the moment of answering. Edit it in a minute, read it in plain English, and keep it when the model underneath changes. Baked-in weights give you none of that, and they cannot tell you why they said a thing. Nobody ruled this out; the reasoning did. A voice test that fails puts it back on the table with evidence attached.

DECISION 02No full-corpus graph.

2.4 terabytes is not 2.4 terabytes of writing. Most of it is the artwork that gets linked into newsletters and landing pages, plus tens of thousands of spreadsheets. Catalogue the art, load the spreadsheets, and let a model read only the part that is actually prose, and only the years anyone asks about. Nobody knows the real number yet, which is why counting is the first gate rather than an afterthought.

The ten decisions behind it

Tap a number to light up the piece it belongs to in the drawing above.

Two things we talked about and are not doing

Both were live options. Both are struck, for reasons that should be argued with rather than assumed.

Struck

Fine-tuning a model on the house voice

It sounds like the serious version and it is the fragile one. Retraining costs real money and weeks, and every edit costs it again. You cannot read the result, cannot ask it why, and cannot lift it onto a better model next year without starting over.

Instead: the same corpus becomes retrieved examples and a written style guide. Cheaper by orders of magnitude, changeable in an afternoon, and it can show its work. If a voice test later proves this is not close enough, fine-tuning comes back on the table with evidence behind it.

Struck

Graphing the whole corpus in one run

$3,500 to $4,000 is the price of the first attempt. The first attempt is never the last one. The instructions will be wrong, the naming will collide, and it will want rerunning. Four passes and it is a $16,000 line item nobody approved.

Instead: structure loads for nothing, prose gets read only where it earns it, and the recent years go first. The bill scales with what people actually ask about, and the older years fill in behind, on a machine that is already paid for.

Where the money goes

Order of magnitude, not a quote. The point is the shape: the large one-off is replaced by work that costs almost nothing, and the running bill has a lid on it.

Is a graph even the right answer?

Mostly no, and the distinction that matters is the one nobody draws: a knowledge graph and GraphRAG are not the same thing. A knowledge graph is assembled out of tables you already own. GraphRAG is a pipeline that pays a model to infer a graph out of raw text, because you have no tables. You have tables.

The test, before you spend

Twenty questions, labelled

Write down the twenty questions people in the building actually ask each other in a week. Real ones, in their own words. Put each into one of the five rows above.

If fewer than five land on the last row, build the first two stores and stop. That costs almost nothing and it will answer most of the twenty. Come back to the graph when there is evidence people need it, not because it is the impressive-sounding option.

The part nobody mentions

Time is the real cost, not money

Reading a whole corpus is measured in days of wall clock, and the first pass is always wrong: the instructions are too vague, the same person appears under four spellings, and half the entities are file paths. You change the prompt and start again.

That loop is what makes it unaffordable, not the invoice. Slicing to one recent year turns a two-week experiment into a two-hour one, and you can be wrong five times before lunch.

A

The big number is a backfill, not a bill

Reading twelve years costs thousands once. Reading tomorrow's memo costs a fraction of a cent. Those are different lines in a budget and they get argued as though they are the same one. Once the history is in, the running cost of staying current is rounding error, and that is the sentence to lead with when the hardware question comes up.
B

A new send is a row, not a re-read

When the next performance record is finished, it lands in the numbers store as rows. The relationships it creates appear on their own, because the links were the table joins all along. No model is involved, nothing is regenerated, and the answer to "does it know about last week" is a timestamp in the ledger rather than a guess.
C

The ledger is the boring part that decides everything

One row per file: where it came from, its hash, when it was seen, which stores hold it, which version of the reader processed it. Without it you cannot answer whether a document is in the system, you re-read things you have already paid for, and you can never safely change the extraction rules. Build it first; it is a week of unglamorous work that makes every later week cheaper.
D

Version the reader, not the corpus

When the instructions improve, and they will, stamp the new version into the ledger and re-read only what matters: the recent slice, or the documents somebody actually queries. A prompt change must never mean starting the whole corpus again. If your design cannot do that, it will quietly stop improving after the first pass, because nobody can afford the second.

Stop the clock

Three things that should halt this

The vocabulary will not converge. If two departments still cannot agree on what a term means at day 30, stop and fix the organisation. No amount of machinery survives it.

Nobody uses the search. If the free, complete, working thing goes unused for a fortnight, the expensive thing will not be used either. That is data, not a setback.

The retention question stays open. Building capture across everyone's inbox before somebody has decided what gets deleted creates a liability faster than it creates value.

Running in parallel, from day one

The unglamorous half

The ledger, the folder watcher, the deletion list, the baseline measurements, the glossary in version control. None of it demonstrates well and all of it is what makes month four cheaper than month one.

Budget it explicitly, or it gets skipped in favour of whatever is more impressive that week, and then the second pass costs as much as the first one did.

Not decided yet

None of these is an engineering unknown. Three of the four are governance, which means they are somebody's decision rather than a thing to be found out, and the sequence on Plate 03 stalls without them.

  1. Where does the donor file actually live?

    Nobody has named the platform. If it holds card tokens, that changes what the sandbox has to be, and it is the one thing here that is not recoverable if it goes wrong.

  2. What did the petition signers and donors agree to?

    Appending bought wealth data to a donor record, and reusing petition signatures for analysis, are both secondary uses of personal data. What the sign-up language and privacy policy actually permit has to be read before the pipeline is built.

  3. How long does routine material live?

    The people closest to the legal risk want a 30-to-60-day floor on what could end up in a deposition. The people closest to the archive do not want to delete anything. Organisation-wide capture cannot be designed until someone above both of them decides.

  4. Does the graph ever hold donor records?

    This design says no, permanently, and pays a real price in analytical power for it. That is a choice worth making deliberately rather than by default.
